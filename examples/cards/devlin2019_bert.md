## §1 One-liner

Pre-trains a deep bidirectional Transformer encoder with a masked-language-model objective plus next-sentence prediction, then fine-tunes one output layer per downstream task, reporting state-of-the-art results on eleven NLP tasks.

## §2 Question

Whether a **bidirectional** pre-trained representation — as opposed to the left-to-right language-model pre-training then in use — is what the downstream gains come from, and whether fine-tuning with a single added layer is enough to transfer it.

## §3 Prior work

[as-claimed] Two strategies are named: feature-based (ELMo) and fine-tuning (OpenAI GPT). The paper's stated objection to both is architectural — unidirectional pre-training restricts what the encoder can see at each layer. The quantitative comparison is Table 1 and Table 2, and the card reproduces the rows it cites.

Mechanism in one paragraph: a Transformer encoder stack over WordPiece tokens, with segment and position embeddings summed into the input; pre-training optimises masked-token prediction plus next-sentence classification jointly; fine-tuning adds one task-specific output layer and updates all parameters. The architectural difference from GPT is only the attention mask — the paper says so explicitly, which is what makes the comparison in Table 1 an argument about bidirectionality rather than about capacity.

`[qualitative]` The paper's central comparison is BASE vs LARGE at equal fine-tuning: LARGE wins on every GLUE task in Table 1 and by 2.5 points on the average, which the paper attributes to model size. Because each cell is a single fine-tuned model selected on the dev set — with several random restarts for LARGE on small datasets — the comparison is between selected maxima, not between means.

**Cross-check.** Table 1 (p.6) was rebuilt from word coordinates and compared cell by cell with the text-layer rows after the column-bleed switch; the nine value columns agree and only the last row label is offset. Tables 2-4 (p.7) were read from the text layer without a rebuild: they straddle a column break in the original layout and the rebuild would have to join two x-ranges, which was not attempted.

## §6 Limitations

### Author-stated

- Fine-tuning BERT_LARGE "was sometimes unstable on small datasets", so several random restarts were run and the best dev model selected (p.6 §4.1, footnote 9).
- The GLUE "Average" column has WNLI removed, so it is not the official GLUE score (p.6 Table 1 caption).
- Pre-training cost: 4 days on 16 TPU chips (base) or 64 chips (large) (p.13 App. A) — a reproduction barrier the authors state plainly.

### Reader-observed

- **Two "averages" and two baselines are printed on the same page, and the abstract mixes them.** 82.1 (table average, WNLI excluded) against a 75.1 baseline gives the 7.0 points claimed in §4.1; 80.5 (official leaderboard score) against a 72.8 baseline gives the 7.7 points claimed in the abstract. Both are correct, neither is the number the other sentence used, and a reader comparing "7.0" and "7.7" would reasonably conclude one of them is wrong.
- **Selection on the dev set is part of the test number here.** LARGE's GLUE cells are the best of several restarts chosen on dev, so the reported test figures are maxima of a selection procedure whose spread is not reported.
- **The headline "outperforms all systems on all tasks" is scoped by the caption.** Table 1 excludes entries that use BERT as a component, which is the right exclusion but is stated in the caption rather than beside the claim.
- **SWAG is below human performance in the paper's own table** — 86.3 against 88.0 for five human annotations. The paper reports the row; it does not call attention to it.

## §7 Transferable

- **One architectural knob, one comparison.** BASE and LARGE differ only in size, and the paper's argument rests on that pair being otherwise identical. Copying this discipline is what makes an ablation readable a year later.
- **Put the reproduction-critical hyperparameters in one appendix block.** Batch, steps, optimizer, schedule, dropout, activation, hardware — six lines, one paragraph, nothing scattered. Four of this card's §4 rows come from that single paragraph.
- **Say what the metric is not.** The caption that separates "Average" from "official GLUE score" costs one sentence and prevents the single most common misquote of this paper.
- **Report the human ceiling in the same table.** SWAG's 88.0 row makes the remaining gap visible without a separate discussion section.
